The Arabic Word — الألتراس

الألتراس Al-Ultraas A direct Arabic transliteration of "ultras" — used identically across Tunisia, Morocco, Egypt, and the wider Arab world

The Arabic language adopted the word "ultras" through transliteration — converting the Latin sounds into Arabic script: الألتراس (Al-Ultraas). The definite article "Al" (الـ) is prefixed in Arabic, giving the term a weight and formality it carries in every stadium from Tunis to Cairo. There is no separate Arabic word for the concept — the culture was imported whole, and the name came with it.

Across social media, banners, chants, and fan forums throughout the Arab world, the term is written and spoken the same way: الألتراس. It signals immediately that you are part of an organized, committed, principled supporter culture — not just a fan, but something more.

Tunisia: The Pioneer

It began in Tunisia in 1995. When Club Africain supporters formed African Winners, they did not simply create a fan club — they created the first ultra group in the entire Arab world and the African continent.

Tunisia's exposure to Italian football through satellite television in the 1990s was the catalyst. Young Tunisian fans watched Serie A, absorbed the culture of the Italian curva, and recognized in it something that spoke to them: organized, passionate, visually spectacular, and fiercely loyal support. They took the model and made it their own.

Within a decade, Tunisian stadiums had been transformed. The Stade Olympique de Radès went from a venue with ordinary crowd noise to one of the most atmospherically intense grounds in Africa — with multiple organized ultra groups competing to produce the loudest chants and the most spectacular tifos.

"Tunisia was the gateway through which ultra culture entered the Arab world. What began in Tunis in 1995 had spread across North Africa and the Middle East within fifteen years." — Research on ultra culture in North Africa, FTDES

How the Culture Spread

The journey of ultra culture through the Arab world followed television, the internet, and personal contact between fans across borders.

1995 Tunisia

African Winners founded — the first ultra group in the Arab world and Africa. Tunisia becomes the cradle of Arab ultra culture.

2002 Tunisia (2nd wave)

Second-generation groups emerge, including Ultras L'Emkachkhines of Espérance. The Tunisian movement consolidates and diversifies.

2005 Morocco

Ultra culture formally arrives in Morocco. Winners 2005 (Wydad, Casablanca) and Green Boys (Raja) are among the first organized groups.

2000s Egypt

Ultras Ahlawy (Al-Ahly) and Ultras White Knights (Zamalek) emerge as Egypt's dominant groups — among the largest and most politically influential in the Arab world.

2000s Algeria

Groups form around major clubs including USM Alger and ES Sétif, completing North Africa's ultra landscape.

2010s Wider Arab World

Ultra culture reaches Jordan, Lebanon, Saudi Arabia, and beyond — carried by social media, satellite sports coverage, and North African diaspora communities.

Characteristics of Arab Ultra Culture

Arab ultra culture adopted the core Italian framework but developed its own distinct characteristics, shaped by the social, political, and cultural context of the region.

  • ●
    Bilingual chants. Arab ultras mix Arabic with French (in the Maghreb) or English. Chants carry the same emotional weight as Italian originals but in the rhythms of Arabic poetry and dialect.
  • ●
    Intense visual culture. Tifos in Arab stadiums rival anything produced in Europe — massive hand-painted banners, professional-grade choreography, and creative flare sequences have become a point of regional pride.
  • ●
    Social function. In societies where public assembly can be restricted, the stadium and the ultra curve became one of the few spaces where young people could gather, organize, and express collective identity freely.
  • ●
    The Arab Spring connection. Egypt's ultras became globally known during the 2011 revolution. Groups like Ultras Ahlawy and Ultras White Knights used their organizational skills — the same skills learned in the curva — on the streets of Cairo. The stadium had trained them for collective action.
  • ●
    Pan-North African brotherhood. Unlike European ultra culture, which is often intensely local, Arab ultra groups across Tunisia, Morocco, and Algeria maintain strong cross-border friendships — connections that transcend national rivalries in favor of a shared regional identity.
  • ●
    The "Winners" network. The shared name "Winners" — used by Club Africain's Ultras Africain Winners (Tunisia, 1995) and Wydad's Winners 2005 (Morocco, 2005) — is an example of this cross-border solidarity. Red clubs, red jerseys, shared identity, shared passion.

Tunisia: The Ultra Nation

Tunisia has a richer ultra culture, relative to its population, than almost any country in the Arab world. With four major organized groups across its top clubs and a tradition stretching back to 1995, Tunisian football stadiums are among the most atmospherically intense on the continent.

The Stade Olympique de Radès — capacity 60,000 — regularly fills to produce scenes that would not look out of place in Naples, Genoa, or Rome. The Virage Nord and the opposing curves erupt with color, smoke, and sound from the first minute to the last.

Beyond atmosphere, Tunisian ultras have been studied by academics and journalists as examples of how organized supporter culture can function as civil society — teaching organizational skills, collective responsibility, and community identity to an entire generation of young people.

"In Tunisia, the ultra movement is not just about football. It is a form of social organization — perhaps the most vibrant form of youth civic engagement the country has ever produced." — FTDES (Tunisian Forum for Economic and Social Rights) report on ultra groups
