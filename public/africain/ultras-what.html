What is Ultras?

Beyond ordinary fandom. Beyond the final whistle. A culture, a code, and a complete way of seeing the game.

The Word Itself

ULTRA Latin — "Beyond" • Further than • On the other side of

The word "ultra" comes from Latin, meaning "beyond" — further than the ordinary, past the limits of what is expected. An ultra fan does not simply attend a match: they go beyond. Beyond the ordinary spectator. Beyond comfort. Beyond reason. The name itself is a manifesto.

To be an ultra is to commit to something larger than yourself. Your club is not your weekend hobby — it is your identity. The curve is not your seat — it is your territory. The ninety minutes are not entertainment — they are a battleground of passion.

Born in Italy — The 1960s and 70s

The ultra movement was born in Italy during the politically turbulent late 1960s and 1970s, a period known as the "Years of Lead." The first formalized ultra group is widely credited to Sampdoria's "Ultras Tito Cucchiaroni" in 1969. However, proto-ultra groups emerged even earlier: Torino's "Fedelissimi Granata" in 1951, and AC Milan's "Fossa dei Leoni" in 1968.

These groups were inspired partly by the political passion and collective action of the era — student movements, labor unions, civic organizations. They borrowed the language of political movements — banners, organized chanting, mass choreography — and redirected it into the football stadium. The curve became their parliament.

"The ultra phenomenon in Italy arose in the context of a society in turmoil — and the stadium became one of the few places where young men could organize, express collective identity, and feel genuinely powerful." — Academic analysis of Italian ultra culture

Spreading Across Europe

Through the 1970s and 1980s, ultra culture spread from Italy to Spain (where fans became "ultras" or "boixos"), France (the "kop" culture in Marseille and Paris), Germany, and Eastern Europe. By the 1990s, it had crossed the Mediterranean and taken root in North Africa — beginning in Tunisia.

The Ultra Philosophy

Being an ultra is not about the result on the scoreboard. It is about the atmosphere you create, the loyalty you demonstrate, and the identity you build around your club. The ultra code is built on a set of core principles shared by groups from Naples to Tunis to Casablanca.

🔥

The Curve

The ultra group owns a specific stand — called the "curva" in Italian or "virage" in French. It is sovereign territory. Everything happens here.

🏁

Tifo

Hand-painted giant banners, colored card sections, flag cascades, and flare smoke — unveiled the moment the players emerge from the tunnel. Tifo is the group's visual voice.

🎤

The Capo

The leader who stands at the front of the curve, back to the pitch, facing their own fans — directing chants and choreo with arms, megaphone, and voice.

📶

Chants

Ultras compose original chants — set to music, poetry, and rhythm. Their chants narrate the club's history, its glory, and its enemies. Ninety minutes of song.

✈

Away Days

Ultras follow their team everywhere — home, away, across the continent. The hardship of travel to a hostile away ground is a badge of honor.

🤝

Brotherhood

The group is a family. Members cover each other's costs, share resources, and stand together. Friendships with allied groups ("gemellaggio") cross city and national borders.

The Code of the Curve

Every authentic ultra group operates by a set of unwritten but deeply respected rules. These are the principles that separate true ultras from ordinary fans.

  • 1
    Independence from the club. Ultras groups fund themselves. They refuse corporate sponsorship, club subsidies, or any arrangement that could compromise their freedom to criticize club management.
  • 2
    No politics inside the curve. The classic ultra tradition holds that the curve is politically neutral. Whatever your views outside the stadium, inside the curve you are united only by the club.
  • 3
    You support through defeat. The mark of a true ultra is loyalty in relegation, in crisis, in humiliation. Fair-weather fans are unwelcome in the curve.
  • 4
    The tifo is sacred. Months of work — painting, folding, building — go into a tifo display that lasts 90 seconds. It is made with pride and presented with ceremony.
  • 5
    Respect the alliance, honor the rivalry. Friend groups ("gemellaggio") are treated as brothers. Enemy groups face the full force of the curve's contempt — inside the stadium, through banners, through songs.
  • 6
    Never sit down. The curve stands for ninety minutes, plus injury time, plus the celebration. Sitting is for the grandstand. The curve lives on its feet.
"Being an ultra is not a hobby. It is a way of life — a commitment that goes beyond the final whistle, beyond the season, beyond everything ordinary. When you enter the curve, you leave the ordinary world behind."
